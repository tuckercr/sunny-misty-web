Is the pavement too hot for my dog?

On a sunny day, the pavement can be far hotter than the air. A pleasant 25°C (77°F) afternoon can leave dark asphalt above 50°C (122°F), hot enough to burn the pads of a dog's paws. Here's how to tell, what to do about it, and how to look after paws in winter too.

The seven-second test. Hold the back of your hand flat on the pavement for seven seconds. If it's too hot for your hand, it's too hot for paws.

Why the pavement is hotter than the air

The forecast tells you the temperature of the air in the shade. Dark asphalt sits in the sun and soaks it up, so in strong sun it can run 25 to 30°C (45 to 55°F) hotter than the air. Roughly, in strong midday sun:

Air temperatureDark asphalt in full sun
20°C (68°F)45 to 50°C (113 to 122°F)
25°C (77°F)50 to 55°C (122 to 131°F)
30°C (86°F)55 to 60°C (131 to 140°F)
35°C (95°F)60 to 65°C (140 to 149°F)

These are estimates. Cloud, shade and a breeze bring the numbers down; a still, cloudless day pushes them up.

So it isn't only heatwaves. A mild, bright day in spring can be enough, and the air feeling nice is no guide to what's underfoot.

When it's hottest

Pavement heats up quickly in the morning sun and gives the heat back slowly. It's usually at its hottest in the afternoon and can stay warm well into the evening, even after the sun has gone down. Early morning, after a night to cool off, is the safest time on a hot day. More in our guide to the best time to walk your dog.

Which surfaces get hottest

  • Asphalt and tarmac are the worst: dark, and they hold the heat.
  • Concrete and paving stones are usually a little cooler, but still hot enough to hurt in full sun.
  • Artificial grass, sand and metal (manhole covers, car park ramps, the bed of a truck) can get very hot too.
  • Real grass and shade stay far cooler. Shaded pavement is much cooler than the same pavement in the sun.

Keeping paws safe on hot days

  • Walk early. First thing in the morning is best. Late evening is next, once the pavement has had time to cool.
  • Stick to grass and shade. Choose parks and shady streets over open roads and car parks.
  • Keep it short in the heat. On the hottest afternoons, a quick potty break on the grass is enough.
  • Try dog boots. They help, but most dogs need some practice at home before they'll walk happily in them.
  • Bring water, for drinking and for cooling paws if you get caught out.

Puppies, older dogs and dogs with thin or sensitive pads may burn more easily, so take extra care with them.

Signs of burnt paws

  • Limping, or refusing to walk on
  • Licking or chewing at their paws
  • Pads that look darker or redder than usual
  • Blisters, or loose or missing bits of pad

What to do

Get your dog off the hot surface, onto grass or into the shade, and carry them if you can. Cool the paws with cool (not icy) water. Keep them clean, and stop your dog licking them. Then call your vet: burns can be worse than they first look, and your vet can tell you what to do next.

Paws in winter

Cold brings its own problems for paws. For the rest of the dog, see how cold is too cold to walk a dog.

  • Salt and grit. The salt and de-icers spread on pavements can dry out and crack pads, and they're not good to lick. Wipe paws with a damp cloth when you get home.
  • Ice. Ice can cut pads, and frozen pavement is hard on paws on a long walk. Keep to snow or grass where you can.
  • Snowballs between the toes. Snow can pack into balls in the fur between a dog's toes. Keeping that fur trimmed helps.
  • Boots and paw balm help in a hard winter, especially on salted streets.

How Sunny & Misty helps

Its Paw Check estimates the temperature of dark pavement in direct sun, hour by hour, from the air temperature and how strong the sun is.

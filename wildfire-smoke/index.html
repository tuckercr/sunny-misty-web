Is it safe to walk my dog in wildfire smoke?

Smoke from wildfires can drift hundreds of miles, and the fine particles in it get deep into the lungs: ours and our dogs'. A dog out on a walk breathes harder than we do and is closer to the ground. Here's how to tell when the air is too bad for a walk, and what to do instead.

The short answer. Check the air quality index (AQI) before you go. Up to 100, walk as normal. From 101 to 150, keep walks short and gentle for older dogs, flat-faced breeds and dogs with heart or breathing problems. Above 150, keep it to quick potty breaks for every dog, and stay in when it's very bad.

How smoke affects dogs

The harm in smoke is mostly the tiny particles called PM2.5, small enough to pass deep into the lungs and into the blood. They irritate the eyes, nose, throat and lungs, and they make heart and breathing problems worse. A dog running about takes in far more air, and more smoke, than one strolling.

Some dogs feel it sooner:

  • Flat-faced breeds, like pugs, bulldogs, French bulldogs and boxers, who already work hard to breathe.
  • Older dogs and puppies.
  • Dogs with heart or lung problems, such as heart disease, a collapsing windpipe or bronchitis.

Cats are affected too, especially those with asthma. If your cat goes outside, keep them in while the air is smoky.

When to walk, by the air quality index

The US air quality index runs from 0 to 500. Europe uses its own scale from "Good" to "Extremely poor", and the bands line up roughly like this:

A rule of thumb, not a hard line. If you can smell smoke or see haze, the air isn't good, whatever the number says.

Keeping dogs safe on smoky days

  • Check the air before you go, and check it hour by hour. Smoke comes and goes with the wind, so a clearer spell can open up later in the day.
  • Keep it short and slow. A gentle stroll beats a run. Skip fetch, jogging and the dog park until the air clears.
  • Play indoors instead. Puzzle feeders, sniffing games and short training sessions use up energy without heavy breathing.
  • Keep the smoke out. Close windows and doors, and run an air purifier with a HEPA filter if you have one. Set the car's air to recirculate.
  • Keep water handy. Smoke dries out the throat.
  • Wipe off ash. If ash is falling, wipe your dog's paws and coat when you come in, so they don't lick it off.

Other kinds of bad air

  • Ozone builds up on hot, sunny afternoons, especially in cities, so mornings tend to be cleaner. More in our guide to the best time to walk your dog.
  • Dust storms bring coarse particles that irritate eyes and airways. Treat them like smoke.
  • Pollution from traffic is worst along busy roads. Parks and quiet streets are better for a walk.

Signs the air is getting to your dog

  • Coughing, gagging or wheezing
  • Sneezing, or a runny nose
  • Red, watery or itchy eyes
  • Tiring quickly, or seeming slow and flat
  • Breathing hard, or with their mouth open, at rest

If you see these, take your dog inside to cleaner air and let them rest. Trouble breathing, blue or pale gums, or collapse is an emergency: call your vet straight away.

When is the best time to walk my dog?

It depends on the weather more than the clock. In summer the best walk is usually early in the morning; in winter it's the middle of the day. Rain, wind, storms and smoky air can all move it too. Here's how to pick your moment.

The short answer. On hot days, walk early in the morning or late in the evening, and keep to grass and shade. On cold days, walk around midday, when it's warmest. Whatever the season, skip thunderstorms and very smoky air, and time walks between showers.

Walking your dog in hot weather

Dogs cool themselves mostly by panting, which works far less well than sweating does for us. On a hot day they can overheat quickly, especially if they're running about.

  • Go early. The first hour or two after sunrise is the coolest part of a hot day, for the air and for the pavement.
  • Or go late. Evenings are next best, but the pavement holds the day's heat well after the air cools, so check it before you set off. Our paw safety guide explains why.
  • Skip the middle of the day. From late morning to late afternoon, keep outings to short potty breaks on the grass.
  • Watch the humidity. Sticky air makes panting work less well, so a humid day can be harder on a dog than a hotter, drier one.
  • Slow down. Shorter, gentler walks with shade and water beat a long run on a hot day.

Flat-faced breeds (pugs, bulldogs, French bulldogs), older dogs, overweight dogs, puppies and dogs with thick coats get too hot sooner, so take extra care with them.

Signs your dog is too hot

  • Heavy, fast panting that doesn't settle when they rest
  • Lots of drooling, or a very red tongue and gums
  • Slowing down, lying down or refusing to go on
  • Stumbling, confusion, vomiting or collapse

Heatstroke is an emergency. Get your dog into the shade, cool them with cool (not icy) water, offer small sips to drink, and call your vet straight away.

Walking your dog in cold weather

  • Go in the middle of the day, when it's warmest and brightest.
  • Keep walks shorter when it's bitterly cold, and keep moving.
  • A coat helps small dogs, short-haired dogs, thin dogs and older dogs, who feel the cold sooner.
  • Mind the paws. Salt, grit and ice are hard on pads. Wipe paws when you get home. There's more in our paw safety guide.

Signs your dog is too cold: shivering, lifting their paws off the ground, slowing down, or trying to turn for home. Head back and warm up. Our guide to how cold is too cold to walk a dog has more.

Rain, wind, storms and the dark

Rain

Most dogs don't mind a bit of rain, but a soaking walk is no fun for anyone. Showers often come and go, so check the radar and the hourly forecast and slip out in a dry spell. Keep a towel by the door.

Wind

A breeze is fine. In strong winds, branches and loose things can come down, and small dogs get buffeted about. From about a near gale (around 50 km/h or 31 mph), it's worth waiting for it to ease. In a full gale, keep it to a quick potty break.

Thunderstorms

Don't walk in a thunderstorm. Lightning is a real risk out in the open, and many dogs are frightened of thunder: a scared dog can slip a collar and bolt. If storms are forecast, walk before they arrive.

Smoke and air pollution

Wildfire smoke, pollution and ozone affect dogs too, especially flat-faced breeds, older dogs and dogs with heart or breathing problems. When the air is unhealthy, keep trips outside short. Ozone usually builds up through hot, sunny afternoons, so mornings tend to be cleaner.

The dark

In winter, the best hours can be short. If you walk in the dark, a reflective lead or a light on your dog's collar helps drivers and cyclists see you both.

A rule of thumb for each season

SeasonUsually bestWatch out for
SummerEarly morning, then late eveningHot pavement, heat, humidity, afternoon storms and ozone
WinterAround middayCold, ice and salt, wind, short daylight

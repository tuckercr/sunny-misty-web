How cold is too cold to walk a dog?

It depends on the dog as much as the thermometer. A husky can happily trot through a frosty morning that leaves a chihuahua shivering at the end of the road. Here's a rough guide to how cold is too cold, which dogs feel it first, and how to keep winter walks safe.

The short answer. Most dogs are fine above about 7°C (45°F). Below freezing, keep walks shorter and put a coat on small, thin and short-haired dogs. Below about −7°C (20°F), keep it to quick trips for most dogs, and go by what it feels like, wind and all, not just the temperature.

A rough guide by temperature

Go by the "feels like" temperature, which counts the wind. A breezy 2°C can feel well below freezing.

Feels likeFor most dogs
Above 7°C (45°F)Fine for nearly every dog.
0 to 7°C (32 to 45°F)Fine for most. Small, thin, short-haired, young and older dogs may want a coat.
−7 to 0°C (20 to 32°F)Keep walks shorter. Coats for small and short-haired dogs, and watch the paws.
Below −7°C (20°F)Quick trips for most dogs. Even thick-coated breeds can get frostbite on a long walk.

This is a rule of thumb, not a hard line. Wet fur, wind and standing about make the cold bite much harder; sunshine and keeping moving help.

Which dogs feel the cold first

  • Small dogs, like chihuahuas, toy poodles and Yorkshire terriers, lose heat quickly and are close to the cold ground.
  • Thin and short-haired dogs, like greyhounds, whippets, boxers and Staffies, have little fat or fur to keep warm.
  • Puppies and older dogs can't hold their body heat as well.
  • Dogs with health problems, such as arthritis, heart disease or diabetes, often struggle more in the cold. Stiff joints get stiffer too.

Thick, double-coated breeds, like huskies, malamutes, Bernese mountain dogs and Newfoundlands, are built for it and often love the snow. They still have limits, especially with ears, paws and tails.

Keeping winter walks safe

  • Walk in the middle of the day, when it's warmest and brightest. More in our guide to the best time to walk your dog.
  • Shorter and more often beats one long walk when it's bitter out. Keep moving rather than standing about.
  • A coat or jumper helps the dogs above. It should cover the back and chest and fit well, and come off indoors.
  • Mind the paws. Salt, grit and ice are hard on pads. Wipe paws when you get home, and think about boots or paw balm. There's more in our paw safety guide.
  • Dry them off. A wet dog gets cold far faster. Towel off snow and rain when you get in.
  • Keep off frozen ponds and lakes. Ice can give way under a dog, and people who go in after them get into trouble too. Keep dogs on a lead near water.
  • Watch out for antifreeze. It tastes sweet to dogs and is very poisonous. Keep them away from puddles on driveways and car parks, and call your vet straight away if you think they've licked some.
  • Be seen in the dark. Winter walks often start or end in the dark, so a reflective lead or a light on the collar helps drivers see you both.

Signs your dog is too cold

  • Shivering
  • Lifting their paws off the ground, or limping
  • Slowing down, whining, or trying to turn for home
  • Hunching up with their tail tucked in

If you see these, head home and warm up. Most of the time that's all it takes.

Hypothermia and frostbite

Hypothermia is when a dog's body gets dangerously cold. Watch for heavy shivering that then stops, weakness, stiff muscles, slow breathing and a dog that seems sleepy or confused. Frostbite usually hits the ears, tail and paws: the skin looks pale or grey and feels cold and hard, and later turns red and sore.

Both are emergencies. Get your dog somewhere warm, wrap them in dry blankets, and call your vet. Warm them gently: don't rub frostbitten skin, and don't use hot water or a heater straight on it.

How Sunny & Misty helps

Sunny & Misty is a free weather app for iPhone, iPad and Android. When it's cold, Sunny and Misty put on their scarves, and the walk card goes by what it feels like out there, wind and all:

When it feels likeThe walk card
3°C (37°F) or moreCounts the hour as good for a walk, if the rest of the weather agrees.
Below 3°C (37°F)Waits for a warmer hour: "Too chilly out."
0°C (32°F) or lessPotty breaks only: "Too cold for a long walk."

On a cold day it looks through the rest of the day for the warmest good spell, and if there isn't one, it says so. Paw Check warns when the pavement is below freezing, and after rain or snow it says "Icy, watch for salt."
